# Episode 1 "Contour"

In the morning, a circle. At night, the circle rises into the sky.

I wake up. At the set hour. I wash my face, brush my teeth, pour a glass of water down my throat. This is where the frame of a day begins.

In Ringa, nothing is left undecided. Meals arrive from the automatic cooker at the set hour. If you clean, dust is dancing again by morning, but that, too, is more or less decided. Work. Training. Study. Everything is written on the timetable, and the day of a person living here flows on as if tracing that band.

Names, in Ringa, mostly do not exist. You are assigned a date of birth, and that is all. Everyone is called by a number and a nickname. I am "Maru." —Why? Because I always draw circles.

When the morning routine is done, I turn to a wall. The wall of the Inner Ring. Everyone's territory. Here, every morning, one circle. On the white, rough wall, I entrust this day of mine to the shape of a circle. When I finish it, after a while it begins to glow. When night comes it rises into the sky and is drawn into the sphere at the center, that one.

In other words, our days are eaten by the sphere, every single night.

My circle always stops at eight-tenths. Just before the line reaches back to where it began, my left hand stops on its own. Just before fullness, I hold, hard. In Ringa, that is the rule. Meals eight-tenths. Work eight-tenths. Nothing may be filled. —They say that if you draw a full circle, the sphere will look at it that night.

It is not for anyone's sake, and no one asks about it. The rule stays a rule: when morning comes, draw a circle; when night comes, it rises.

My work is the crops of the Outer Ring. Watering, harvesting, recording. Ringa has no four seasons. The sky of Holo is always clear, and rain never falls. Even so the crops grow, and we live by eating them.

When work ends, in the evening, I go to the hot spring in the Inner Ring.

Across the steam sat an old woman. Soaked in the water with a bent back, she was looking up at the sky. ...No, —not the sky. Something lower down. The sphere, at the center.

"Do you look at that every day?"

"That's the place where you can see it best."

When I sat down beside her, the old woman gave her name: "Shizuku." A human being with a name, rare in Ringa. —One of the people called Old Ones. One of the few survivors who lived through the war that ended ten years ago.

"And your name?"

"...Maru. Because I draw circles."

"What a good name."

The old woman laughed in a voice like a half-broken radio.

"Maru. Someday there will come a day when you want to fill your circle."

I wondered what she meant. Filling it is forbidden.

Night. At the set hour, I get into bed.

Just before I fall asleep, I always notice one thing. —The "fragments" of my dreams are completely gone. Nobody in Ringa remembers their dreams on waking. The sphere harvests eight-tenths of the dream before it becomes a memory. That too is the rule. But my dreams vanish more than other people's. Nothing is left. I forget even that I dreamed, again and again.

In the middle of the night, I woke.

Outside the window. Toward the boundary. In the direction of the bridge, —

I heard laughter. Distorted, like radio noise, but unmistakably a voice I know. Very high, and bright. Haru's way of laughing.

Haru is gone. Seven years ago he did Rindō. He graduated. He walked into the shadow of the sphere at the center and was drawn in. My brother.

"...That's strange."

Ringa even manages laughter, keeps it at a constant.

Morning. I rise at the set hour. I wash my face, brush my teeth, drink water.

I face the wall and draw one circle. —I stop at eight-tenths.

The circle will reach only eight-tenths tomorrow, too. That was the rule, and it was my habit.
